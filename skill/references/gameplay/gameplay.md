# Gameplay

Deckbuilding answers "what should be in this deck." This book answers "given this board, this hand, and this phase, what's the right move." They fail in different ways: a deckbuilding mistake shows up over many games as a structural weakness, a gameplay mistake is a single wrong decision in a single turn. Don't reach for deckbuilding-methodology language ("this deck wants to be aggro") when the actual question is a live tactical one ("should this unit attack now or wait one more turn").

Base mechanics below (turn phases, the chain, combat resolution) are stable enough to state directly. Anything that depends on current card text or current legality is a different question — check it against the live sources in the library's top-level "route, don't snapshot" section, not against memory of a specific card's effect.

## Turn structure: the six phases

A turn belongs to one player (the Turn Player); the other is the Off-Turn Player for that turn. Control passes after End of Turn completes. Six phases, in order, every turn:

1. **Awaken** — ready all Runes, Units, and Gear you control (undo exhaustion from last turn).
2. **Beginning** — score one point for each Battlefield you control uncontested; any "start of turn" triggered abilities resolve here.
3. **Channel** — draw 2 Rune cards from your Rune Deck into your Base, ready. (The second player channels 3 on their very first turn, to offset going second.)
4. **Draw** — draw one card from your Main Deck; your Rune Pool (unspent Energy/Power) empties at the end of this phase — resources don't carry over. If your Main Deck is empty when you'd draw, you don't just skip the draw and hand your opponent a point instead — the game runs a specific four-step **Burn Out** sequence (Core Rules 431.2): complete as much of the draw as currently possible (zero cards, since the deck is empty), recycle your entire trash back into your Main Deck, your opponent scores 1 point, and only then do you complete the original action — you still draw a card, just after paying the point and refilling your deck from your own trash first.
5. **Main** — the phase with actual decisions: play Units/Spells/Gear, activate abilities, move Units, initiate Showdowns and Combat. No fixed action limit — resources and legality are the only constraints.
6. **End of Turn** — temporary effects expire, Marked Damage clears from all Units, the Rune Pool empties again, turn passes.

Players alternate turns until someone reaches the win condition. Because scoring happens in Beginning (not at the end of a turn), a Battlefield you're about to lose still needs to be defended *before* your opponent's next Beginning phase, not just "at some point" — the scoring clock is tighter than it first looks.

## The chain, priority, and focus

This is the response/interaction system — the part of the rules most likely to get misplayed by someone reasoning from a different TCG's stack rules.

**Two chain states, plus a Showdown-specific restriction that's easy to misread as describing the Open state.** *Open* (Core Rules 310.1.a, the "Neutral Open" state): no active chain. The player with priority can play **any** card type or activate **any** ability, not only Actions — playing one begins a chain. *Closed*: a chain is active. From here, players may only add *Reactions*, each stacking on top of the last. Separately, whenever a Showdown is underway (Core Rules 308.1.a), only Action- and Reaction-keyword cards/abilities can be played or activated at all — that restriction belongs to the Showdown, not to the Open state generally. The **Action** keyword itself (Core Rules 806.1) is what *grants* a card permission to be played during a Showdown in the first place, including on your opponent's turn — a permission the card carries, not a claim that Action cards are the only thing playable when nothing else is happening.

**Priority vs. focus — different jobs, don't conflate them.** *Priority* governs who can respond within an already-open chain; it alternates between players as the chain builds and resolves. *Focus* governs who can *start* a new chain in the first place, which matters most during a Showdown — only the Focus holder can initiate. In a Showdown, the **attacker gets Focus first**.

**Chain resolution is LIFO, one item at a time — and a single pass-cycle only ever resolves that one top item, never the rest of the chain in one shot** (Core Rules 339.1, 340.1–340.4). When both players pass in sequence without adding anything new, the newest item on the chain resolves completely. Then: if the chain is now empty, play returns to the Open state; if items remain, priority passes to whoever controls the next-newest item, and the whole pass-or-add cycle repeats for that item. A chain with five items on it can take five separate double-pass cycles to fully clear — each cycle only peels off the top layer. Don't read "both players just passed" as "the whole chain just resolved"; it means exactly one more item did.

**Sequencing is the actual skill here.** Acting last in a chain gives you the most information and the most control — you get to see what your opponent committed before you commit further. The practical heuristic community sources converge on: *pass last, spend last* — let the opponent commit resources first whenever the situation allows it. Holding priority to play multiple reactions before passing is legal and often correct when you want to commit several effects as a single, harder-to-answer block rather than telegraphing them one at a time — but it isn't automatic. Tournament Rules 503.9.b: playing a card or activating an ability is *assumed to pass priority* unless you explicitly announce you're retaining it. Skipping that announcement in a sanctioned event can cost you exactly the follow-up play the tactic depends on, even when the underlying read was correct.

*(Source: learnriftbound.gg's chain/priority explainer, cross-checked against app.riftjudge.com rulings on specific priority-in-showdown questions — both community-tier, verify against the Core Rules PDF for a ruling that actually matters in a sanctioned event.)*

## Combat and showdowns

A Showdown starts when ready units move toward a Battlefield during the Main phase — the moving units become **attackers**, units already there become **defenders**.

**Two kinds, and neither is as instant as it sounds.** *Non-Combat Showdown* (Core Rules 344.2, 345, 348): moving onto a Battlefield nobody currently controls does **not** grant control on the spot. It opens a Showdown — the mover gets Focus first — which both players can still respond to before it closes. Only once everyone passes without adding anything does the Showdown close, and control is established then (Core Rules 348.2.a): whoever has units remaining at the Battlefield, if they don't already control it. *Combat Showdown*: moving onto a Battlefield the opponent controls triggers an actual fight, resolved below.

**Combat Showdown resolution, in order:**

1. **Spell window first, not last** (Core Rules 464, the Combat Showdown Step) — before any Might gets summed or any damage assigned, both players can chain spells and abilities that buff, debuff, remove, or otherwise reshape who's even still fighting. This window has to close completely — everyone passes, nothing left to add — before the game moves on. This is where the actual decision-making in a showdown happens, and it happens *before* the arithmetic, not after.
2. **Sum Might** (Core Rules 465.2.a–b) — once the Showdown Step closes, add up each side's combined current Might. Purely arithmetic at this step, and it reflects whatever the spell window just changed.
3. **Assign damage in sequence starting with the Attacker, then deal it simultaneously** (Core Rules 465.2.c) — the Attacker assigns their full Might as damage among the Defender's units first, then the Defender does the same among the Attacker's units. You must assign lethal damage to fully kill one unit before spreading the remainder onto another. Assigning damage is not the same action as dealing it: once both sides finish assigning, all of it is *dealt* at the same moment — sequential assignment, simultaneous impact, two distinct steps.
4. **Determine the result by who's left standing, not by whose Might was higher** (Core Rules 466.3) — there's no rule stating the higher-Might side wins, and no rule that a tie destroys everyone. After damage resolves, whichever side is the *only* one with units remaining at the Battlefield has won; if both sides still have units, if neither does, or if units were Recalled during cleanup, the named result is **No Result** — a real, explicit outcome, not an edge case to wave away. A tied Might total by itself says nothing about who wins; what happened to the actual units does.
5. **Healing, then Deathknell** — surviving units return to full health *before* "when this dies" (Deathknell) effects resolve. This ordering matters for combo timing: don't assume a unit that's about to die can't first trigger something, and don't assume a survivor is still damaged going into the next step.
6. **Claim by remaining presence, not by wiping the board** (Core Rules 466.1.a.2, 466.5) — control goes to whoever has units left at the Battlefield after cleanup, and that's explicitly not limited to destroying the other side. Cleanup can instead **Recall** an Attacker, which changes who's left standing without a unit dying. Destroying every defender is one way to end up as the only side present at the Battlefield; it isn't the only way, and reading "claim" as "you must wipe the board" misreads lines built around movement, Recall, or replacement effects instead of a straight fight.

**Tactical read**: because damage is assigned in sequence — Attacker first — but dealt simultaneously and lethal-first, "trading down" (accepting a worse Might total to bait a spell response, or to force the defender to over-commit a spell that could matter more later) is a real, deliberate line — not just a losing attack. Whether to fight for a Battlefield now versus waiting a turn to build a bigger Might total is the actual decision this section supports; there is no universal answer, it depends on the race (see deckbuilding book's archetype framing — an aggro plan wants to force early Showdowns before the opponent stabilizes, a control plan is fine holding back and forcing the opponent to commit first).

## Mulligan

Mechanic (Core Rules 117.1–117.3): choose up to 2 cards from your opening hand and set them aside, draw that same number of replacement cards, *then* recycle the set-aside cards to the bottom of your Main Deck — in that order, not bottom-then-draw. One mulligan opportunity, and it's all-or-nothing per card you choose — you can't ship the whole hand and start over, and you can't take multiple mulligan passes. The entire skill is in choosing which cards to swap, not in the mechanical action itself.

**What makes a hand keepable** — three things to check for, per community heuristics:

1. **A turn-1 play** — something credible to do on the very first action, typically a unit or a piece of Gear. A hand with no turn-1 play is already behind on tempo in a game where board/Battlefield presence is the win condition (see deckbuilding book).
2. **A turn-2 plan** — a second unit, a removal spell, a combat trick, or a counter — something that keeps developing rather than passing.
3. **A line into the deck's actual win condition** — anything that either advances the current game plan or starts pulling the engine (whatever this specific deck's archetype depends on) toward relevance from turn two onward.

If any of the three is missing from the hand, that's the signal for what to consider swapping — identify the worst 0, 1, or 2 cards against those three criteria and replace exactly those, not a blanket "this hand feels bad" call. Mulligan happens during Setup, before either player has taken a turn — there's no opponent "first play" to react to yet, and no second look once the game is live. What *is* knowable at mulligan time is the opponent's public Legend (always visible) and, in an open-decklist event specifically (Tournament Rules 401.5, run at the Head Judge's discretion for that event or its final rounds), their full decklist — evaluate against whichever of those is actually available, never against plays that haven't happened yet.

*(Source: mulligan-heuristic writeups referenced across multiple community sites, converging on the same three-part framework — treat as settled community consensus rather than one site's opinion, but it's still not official Riot guidance.)*

## Piloting a specific decklist

Someone hands you a finished decklist and asks how to actually play it — not "what does this deck do" (deckbuilding's question) but "given this exact list, what's my sequencing priority, what do I hold, what do I race for." This book is card-agnostic mechanics by design (see "Process discipline" below), so answering this well means combining two things this library already has solidly grounded — deckbuilding's archetype/Legend-construction-logic analysis and this book's own turn/chain/combat mechanics — rather than inventing tactical judgment from nothing. Three tiers, and don't blur them together:

**Tier 1 — mechanically derived, always available, no external lookup needed.** Once a decklist is classified by archetype and Legend construction-logic (per the deckbuilding book's methods), its general sequencing priorities follow directly from mechanics already established in this book. An aggro deck wants to initiate Showdowns early (deckbuilding's archetype framing) — combined with the fact that scoring happens in the Beginning phase and combat damage is assigned lethal-first, sequentially from the Attacker (this book's combat section), the concrete instruction is "contest Battlefields before your opponent's board stabilizes, and don't be shy about a Might-losing trade if it removes what would otherwise out-tempo you next turn." A control deck's "hold back and let the opponent commit first" instinct is the same "pass last, spend last" chain-sequencing principle already stated generically, just applied with this specific deck's removal/answers in mind. A deck whose Legend construction-logic implies a compounding, turn-over-turn resource edge (the Annie pattern, in the deckbuilding book) should generally prioritize surviving to accumulate that edge over racing — that's a synthesis of two already-grounded facts, not a new claim.

**Tier 2 — requires the same cross-referenced-sourcing discipline as a Legend's role-level pattern, and for the same reason.** Anything more specific than the mechanical synthesis above — a particular matchup's plan, a specific sequencing trick against a specific opposing archetype, "hold this card until turn X because of Y" — is exactly the kind of tacit, play-tested knowledge that doesn't come from reading text alone. Get it the same way the deckbuilding book's Annie example does: cross-reference multiple independent real sources (tournament reports, matchup write-ups) and state the pattern, not a single author's opinion. Citing one stray forum post as if it settles a matchup is worse than not answering.

**Tier 3 — say so.** If neither tier produces a real answer — no mechanical synthesis reaches the specific question, and no independently-corroborated source exists — the honest answer is "there's no verified basis for that yet," not a confident-sounding guess dressed up as advice. This is not a hypothetical caution: most decklists for a young, fast-changing card pool won't have Tier 2 material behind them at all, especially outside Global Standard's more-played environment — expect to lean on Tier 1 alone most of the time, and say so rather than papering over the gap.

### Deck primer — the fixed output format

**"How do I actually play this deck" gets the same eight-section shape every time — added 2026-08-18 after an audit found this book had the three-tier method above but no fixed deliverable shape, so the actual answer varied session to session even when the underlying reasoning didn't.** A section that honestly says "no verified basis yet" is a correct primer; a section that's silently skipped, or filled with an invented-sounding specific to avoid an empty section, is not — the point of fixing the shape is to make gaps visible, not to paper over them with confident-sounding prose. Tag every substantive claim with its tier (1/2/3) inline, the same discipline the section above already requires.

1. **Identity** — one sentence: archetype + win condition, sourced from the deckbuilding book's `legend-construction-logic.md` row and its linked `legends/<name>.md` write-up (cite that file's own Tier 2 status — CONFIRMED / partially confirmed / not confirmed / unchecked — don't restate it as settled if the source itself hedges).
2. **Core loop** — 2–4 sentences naming the specific cards/mechanics that actually run the deck, each claim tagged Tier 1 (follows from the Legend/Champion text directly) or Tier 2 (confirmed against real play, cite the source).
3. **Mulligan targets** — apply the Mulligan section's three-part framework (turn-1 play / turn-2 plan / line into the win condition) above, translated into this specific deck's actual cards where a real decklist is available (Tier 2 if the exact cards are sourced), or into the archetype's general shape when only the Legend/Champion analysis is available (Tier 1 — say plainly that specific card picks aren't yet sourced rather than naming cards from ordinary vibes).
4. **Turn-by-turn priorities** — early / mid / closing, tied to two already-grounded facts from this book: Beginning-phase scoring timing (a Battlefield needs defending *before* the next Beginning phase, not "eventually") and the archetype's race posture (aggro/control/combo) from the deckbuilding book.
5. **When to fight, when to hold** — a direct verdict applying this book's chain-priority principle ("pass last, spend last") and combat resolution (lethal-first sequential assignment, simultaneous dealing) to this specific deck's actual threats and answers, not a generic restatement of the archetype label.
6. **2–4 common lines** — named, concrete sequences ("if X is in play by turn N, do Y"), each tagged Tier 1 (mechanically derivable) or Tier 2 (sourced from real reports).
7. **3–5 common mistakes** — the actual failure modes a new pilot of *this* deck falls into. Sourced (Tier 2) where a real source names them; if none exists, say "no verified common-mistake data for this deck yet" rather than generating plausible-sounding ones — invented mistakes are worse than no mistakes section, because they read as authoritative.
8. **Evidence ledger** — one line per major claim above, its tier, and its source if Tier 2. This is the section that keeps the other seven honest; don't compress it into a single "mostly Tier 1" footnote — a reader deciding how much to trust a specific line needs to find its tier without re-deriving it.

Four worked examples follow, one per archetype posture this book actually encounters — Control (Nasus), Aggro (Rengar), Tempo (Kha'Zix — a real-play label the deckbuilding book's aggro/control/combo set doesn't have, reported as-is per that book's own rule), and Combo (Zed's "Miracle Zed"). They were picked for having the most piloting-level real coverage available in their archetype, not for being the best decks — two of the four are rated poorly by current results data, and say so. Read them as demonstrations of the format's honesty discipline (what a section looks like when it's sourced, and what it looks like when it isn't) more than as playbooks; every one has Tier 3 sections.

4. **Turn-by-turn priorities** — Early: play units on curve and "place each temporary +1 where it can change a real attack or defense" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); the deck wants to win the first Showdowns, e.g. Inferna's 3 Might attacking beats the 2-Might early units (riftbound.gg, Tier 2). Because scoring is checked at Beginning phase, a battlefield taken on your turn must survive the opponent's whole turn to pay — so an early conquest is worth an extra unit play to defend it, not just to take it (Tier 1, six-phase structure above). Mid: "Hold several unit plays for one sequenced turn, then commit the boosts only after choosing the battlefield you need to win" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2). Closing: "Keep at least one unit for the next turn rather than emptying your hand into a fight the opponent can already answer" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); cardsrealm.com (2026-04-20) frames the same phase more aggressively — "don't save your resources," make the strongest play every turn, game ends turn 8–9 — the two agree that the pump only exists while units are being played, and differ on how much to keep back (Tier 2, both dated).
5. **When to fight, when to hold** — Fight by default: it's an aggro shell with no holding text anywhere in the kit, so initiate Showdowns before the opponent's board stabilizes (Tier 1, archetype + `legends/rengar.md`). But the "pass last, spend last" principle applies *inside* the Showdown rather than being overridden by the aggro label: Ambush units and Thrill of the Hunt are Reactions, so their best moment is the Combat Showdown Step's spell window, which closes before Might is summed (this book's combat section) — as attacker you have Focus first, so open the fight with the units already there and hold Trophy Hunter / Inferna / Grim Apothecary until the opponent has committed their trick, then land the body and only *then* choose where the +1 goes (Tier 1 synthesis of card text + Showdown-Step ordering; hextechanalytics.com's "choose the target before each play" and "it does not have to go on Inferna" say the same at Tier 2). Two hold verdicts: "stop investing once the combat is won" and keep a unit for the next turn (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); and avoid positions where you're the one blocking — riftstorm.gg (2026-08-03, opponent-side view) says the pump and reaction pieces "are at their best on the attack; they do far less when Rengar is the one defending," which is exactly why opponents try to force it (Tier 2). Since the attacker assigns damage first and lethal-first, know the *post*-Ambush Might total before you start the fight, not just what's on the table (Tier 1).
6. **Common lines** — (a) Going first: Irresistible Faefolk turn 1; turn 2 move it to a battlefield, drag an enemy unit along, then play any Ambush unit to that fight to finish the dragged unit — "they'll lose a unit and practically an entire turn" (cardsrealm.com, 2026-04-20; Tier 2, single source, Unleashed-era but nothing in it has since been banned). (b) Trophy Hunter Ambush into a battlefield the opponent holds, +1 to himself → 7 Might on arrival, forcing a fight where they had none to expect (Tier 1, card text; Tier 2 confirmed cardsrealm.com). (c) Pit Rookie = two buffs from one play (its permanent buff on one unit, Rengar's temporary +1 on another — split across two fights or stack for one), and the real battlefields recycle exactly this play: Star Spring lets the Rookie play pull a spent unit back to base, Emperor's Dais returns the Rookie to hand after conquering so it can be replayed for another trigger (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2). (d) Thrill of the Hunt as a Reaction: replay a unit to any battlefield for free = another Legend trigger, or turn a defender into an attacker to switch on Assault, or move a unit onto an open battlefield during the opponent's turn (hextechanalytics.com; cardsrealm.com — both Tier 2). Trophy Hunter is the only Champion these lines need — Unseen-centred midgame combos in the April write-up are superseded by the verification log's finding that Unseen is essentially unplayed (Tier 2).

**Worked example — Kha'Zix, Voidreaver (Body+Chaos), the Tempo example, chosen because it's the clearest case where the real label sits outside this project's aggro/control/combo vocabulary — the deckbuilding book's rule (verification-log.md, "Standing methodological notes," point 1) is to report the real label rather than force it into the three, so this primer is written as "Tempo," not as the control/attrition read the Tier 1 catalog entry started from — and because its Tier 2 status is NOT CONFIRMED in a specific, useful way: the mechanics were read right, the label and the Champion weighting were wrong, and real coverage exists at piloting level (a Field Guide with early/mid/late and a stated weaknesses paragraph, a French aggregate of 17 real lists, and three dated tournament lists plus one dated deck tech).** Like Nasus and Rengar, this example is deliberately imperfect: no source found writes an explicit keep/ship rule, the label itself is contested between "Tempo" and "midrange" and that is stated rather than resolved, the sourced tournament finishes are mid-table (16th, 26th, 53rd) and Rift Watcher rates the Legend Tier C, and section 7 rests on one guide site's weaknesses paragraph plus two one-line remarks elsewhere.

1. **Identity** — Combat-selection Tempo: win a fight to bank XP, spend that XP (a lasting +1 Might buff, or pulling an exhausted unit home) so the *next* fight is also a win, and manufacture the fights themselves by moving units so an enemy is left alone against Kha'Zix, Mutating Horror. NOT CONFIRMED against the catalog's own Tier 1 read (`legends/khazix.md`, `verification-log.md`, 2026-08-18): the text-only derivation said Control/attrition with Evolving Hunter as a real second lane; real coverage says otherwise — hextechanalytics.com "Archetype Type: Tempo," "a combat-tempo Legend," Mutating Horror "the standard chosen Champion"; riftboundfrance.fr "Corps/Chaos tempo deck powered by experience accumulation" with Mutating Horror in 100% of its 17 tracked lists; riftDecks.com 1,319+ Mutating Horror decks/month vs. ~6 Evolving Hunter (Tier 2). Label caveat, Tier 2, stated not smoothed: riftbound.gg's own guide (Den, 2026-07-05) calls it "a midrange strategy" that "mixes the Hidden and Ambush keywords with a few powerful standalone units," and piltoverarchive.com files one build under "Midrange/Combo" — the disagreement is about pacing vocabulary, not about the plan; every source agrees the plan is engineered 1v1s for XP. "Tempo" is not one of this project's three archetype labels; the deckbuilding book says report the real label in that case, so this primer does, and section 4's race posture is derived from what the sources describe rather than from a label lookup. Real strength, Tier 2: Rift Watcher has the Legend at Tier C, 41.4% win rate, 1.2% play rate (accessed 2026-08-18) — this is a worked example of *method*, not a recommendation to play the deck.
2. **Core loop** — The Legend gains 1 XP whenever you win a combat, and both spends — 1 XP: Buff a unit; 2 XP: move an exhausted friendly unit from a battlefield to its base — Exhaust the Legend, so in practice you get one of the two per turn until Awaken readies it (Tier 1, card text; hextechanalytics.com says the same outright: "Both XP abilities exhaust the Legend, so the buff and rescue usually compete for the same activation," and notes the buff is "lasting +1 Might only if the unit does not already have a buff," Tier 2). Kha'Zix, Mutating Horror (4 Energy + 1 Power, 4 Might, Ambush; UNL — verified against `data/riftcodex_cards_raw.json`) can be played as a Reaction to a battlefield where you have units, and when he attacks or defends with an enemy unit alone there he gets +2 Might this turn and you gain 2 XP (Tier 1, card text). Void Assault (2 Energy + 1 Power, Body/Chaos Signature spell): move a friendly unit, then move an enemy unit — the reminder text itself says that if both go to a battlefield you don't control you're the attacker (Tier 1, card text); it is "the heart of the deck," 3× in 100% of real lists (riftboundfrance.fr; Rift Watcher also 100%), and hextechanalytics.com's stated midgame is "Use Void Assault or a temporary Might boost to create a clean combat, then bank the XP" (Tier 2). Grim Resolve (2 Energy, Action): +3 Might this turn and 2 XP if that unit wins a combat this turn — 88% of lists at 3× (riftboundfrance.fr; Tier 1 card text, Tier 2 presence). The real shell across the four dated lists found — Brett Payne, 16th, RiftAtlas Convergence #2, 2026-08-16 (hextechanalytics.com); CTCG Zrob, #26, Vancouver RQ (mobalytics.gg, page dated 2026-06-05); Utrecht RQ #53 (mobalytics.gg, 2026-06-18); Dan McClure "Kha'Zix Tech," 2026-03-18 (piltoverarchive.com, no event) — is 6 Body + 6 Chaos runes, 3× Void Assault, 3× Irresistible Faefolk, 3× Stacked Deck, 2–3× Star-Crossed, 2–3× Fizz, Trickster, 1–3× Rengar, Trophy Hunter, with Grim Resolve 3× in three of the four (Payne runs Punch First 3× / Rampage / Onslaught as the Might boosts instead); riftboundfrance.fr's aggregate adds Demacian Diplomat 76% ("launches the machine") and the champion mix Fizz 88% / Vex, Apathetic 71% / Rengar 53% / Qiyana, Victorious 41% (Tier 2). Only 1× Mutating Horror main in three of those four lists, with 2 more in the sideboard for Zrob and Utrecht — the Champion is the trigger, not a density plan (Tier 2, lists as published).
3. **Mulligan targets** — **No verified basis yet** for an explicit keep/ship rule from any Kha'Zix source (Tier 3 — hextechanalytics.com has no mulligan section, riftbound.gg's guide "contains no ... detailed mulligan guidance," riftboundfrance.fr gives card presence only). What is sourced: the deck "falls behind if early fights are lost" / "Without winning the first combat, the Legend does nothing at all" (riftbound.gg; riftboundfrance.fr — Tier 2, two independent sites), the early game is "Contest a battlefield where your first combat win is realistic" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2), and the named early units are Irresistible Faefolk (2 Energy, 1 Might), Vex, Apathetic ("fantastic card to develop at a battlefield"), Mister Root ("experience generation"), and Demacian Diplomat (riftbound.gg; riftboundfrance.fr — Tier 2). Translated through this book's three-part frame (Tier 1 synthesis of those sources): turn-1 play = a 2-Energy body — Faefolk, Demacian Diplomat (2 Energy, 2 Might, gain 1 XP on play), Mister Root (2 Energy, 1 Might, 2 XP when it moves to a battlefield), or Traveling Merchant — with a mild preference for the two that put XP on the Legend *before* any combat is won, since that directly patches the "does nothing until the first win" weakness both sites name (Tier 1 card text; the preference itself is this book's inference, not sourced); turn-2 plan = the thing that makes the first fight clean — Grim Resolve or Void Assault, or Stacked Deck to find one (Tier 1 from the sourced loop); line into the win condition = Mutating Horror or Void Assault somewhere in the hand, because the isolated-1v1 trigger *is* the engine (Tier 1). Ship signal, Tier 1: a hand whose plays all start at 4–5 Energy (Vex, Rengar, Horror) with no 2-drop and no trick — it can't win the first fight, and both sources say that's the game. Which specific card to send back from a one-short hand is not sourced.
4. **Turn-by-turn priorities** — Early: "Contest a battlefield where your first combat win is realistic" (hextechanalytics.com); "Win your first combats to accumulate experience and launch your abilities" (riftboundfrance.fr) — both Tier 2 and both saying the same thing: the first fight is chosen for certainty, not for value. Because scoring is checked at Beginning phase, a battlefield taken on your turn only pays if it survives the opponent's turn — and this deck's rescue spend is the wrong tool for that (see section 5), so an early conquest is worth defending with a second unit or a held Grim Resolve, not with the Legend (Tier 1, six-phase structure above). Mid: "Use Void Assault or a temporary Might boost to create a clean combat, then bank the XP"; XP goes to "buff a unit ... or ... rescue an exhausted friendly unit," and "Kha'Zix wins by making each successful fight improve the following one instead of spending XP on marginal value" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); riftboundfrance.fr's midgame is "Qiyana, Victorious and your buffed units take control. Void Assault sets up favorable exchanges" and riftbound.gg's is Rengar, Trophy Hunter as "the muscles in the mid-game" — the two disagree on which 4–5 drop is the midgame body, not on the shape (Tier 2). Race posture: this is neither the aggro "force Showdowns before they stabilize" nor the control "hold and make them commit" from this book's combat section — it's *selective*: fight early and often, but only fights that have been made clean first, and skip the ones that haven't (Tier 1 synthesis; the label being outside the three is exactly why this is derived from the sources rather than looked up). Closing: "Spend XP to secure the final combat or return an exhausted unit to base" (hextechanalytics.com); "Target two battlefields: your ability protects units after conquest, making the double push safer than it appears" (riftboundfrance.fr) — both Tier 2; once ahead, "Void Assault, Punch First alongside the flurry of cheap disruptive spells from the Chaos domain to keep the opponent in check" (riftbound.gg, Tier 2). Known bad matchups, Tier 2, single source: "felt a little too slow to develop against Aurora decks" and "lacked some value to beat an Azir" (riftbound.gg, 2026-07-05, Unleashed-era).
5. **When to fight, when to hold** — Fight only fights you have already made clean; hold everything else. That is the direct application of "pass last, spend last" to this kit: nearly every piece that wins a fight is a Reaction or Action — Mutating Horror (Ambush), Star-Crossed (Reaction: return a friendly and an enemy unit to hand), Grim Resolve and Punch First (Actions) — so as attacker with Focus first, open the Showdown with the unit already there and let the opponent commit their trick before landing the body or the pump (Tier 1, card text + this book's Showdown-Step ordering; hextechanalytics.com's "Ambush lets it enter a battlefield during a fight" is the same idea at Tier 2). One ordering wrinkle no source adjudicates (Tier 1 only, flagged as such): Horror's +2 and 2 XP are locked in when his trigger resolves, so it survives the opponent adding a second unit *afterwards*, but it never fires if they add the second unit *before* he lands — against a hand that can Ambush a body in, the "hold him for last" default has a real cost, and this book has no verified read on which way to lean. Hold verdicts: (i) do not take a marginal first fight to "get started" — "a badly chosen fight can spend a card without creating XP" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2), and set it up with Void Assault instead; (ii) the 2-XP rescue is a Legend Exhaust, so choosing it forfeits that turn's buff (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2) — and because scoring is checked at Beginning phase, rescuing your *only* unit at a battlefield gives up that point before it's scored: rescue trades the battlefield for the unit, so it belongs to units you can't afford to lose, not to every exhausted attacker (Tier 1, card text + six-phase structure). Since the attacker assigns damage first and lethal-first, know the *post*-Ambush, post-Grim-Resolve Might total before committing (Tier 1); the sources' recurring word for what you're checking is "clean" (hextechanalytics.com) — one enemy, alone, whose Might you already beat.
6. **Common lines** — (a) The signature line, Tier 1 (card text) with its shape confirmed Tier 2 (hextechanalytics.com Strengths section: Void Assault "reshapes both sides of a battlefield before combat begins"; riftboundfrance.fr: "Void Assault sets up favorable exchanges"): Void Assault moves one of your units into an enemy-held battlefield and moves one of the two defenders out (or drags a lone enemy into a battlefield where you already have a unit) — either way one enemy is now alone with a friendly unit present, which is exactly the Ambush precondition — then Mutating Horror as a Reaction, +2 Might and 2 XP on the trigger; the Legend adds 1 XP for the win, and Grim Resolve on the same fight adds 2 more, so a single engineered fight can bank up to 5 XP (Tier 1 arithmetic from card text; the 5-XP total is this book's sum, not a sourced number). (b) Faefolk turn 1, then move it to a battlefield and drag a lone enemy along, then Ambush Horror into that fight — same shape the Rengar example sources from cardsrealm.com for Trophy Hunter, here with the isolation bonus on top (Tier 1, card text; riftbound.gg names Forbidding Waste + Faefolk "a great tandem early on" without spelling out the sequence — Tier 2 for the pairing only, and Forbidding Waste's "lone defender loses 2 Might" is why hextechanalytics.com picks it going second, Tier 2). (c) XP before the first win, Tier 1: Demacian Diplomat (1 XP on play) and Mister Root (2 XP on moving to a battlefield) load the Legend so the first buff or rescue is available before combat has been won at all — the sourced weakness paragraphs treat the first win as the only on-switch, and these two cards are the reason 76% of lists run Diplomat (riftboundfrance.fr, Tier 2 presence; the "why" is this book's inference). (d) Star-Crossed as a Reaction to un-crowd an enemy battlefield mid-Showdown (bounce your spare unit and their second defender) so Horror's trigger checks true — Tier 1 derivation only, 94% presence (Rift Watcher, Tier 2) but no source describes it as this line; Isolate (2 Energy, Chaos) does the same job on paper and appears in *none* of the four sourced lists, which is a reason not to recommend it (Tier 2, lists as published). Battlefield picks, Tier 2, one source: Star Spring blind, Sandswept Tomb going first ("when you expect to defend the lane with combat tricks"), Forbidding Waste going second (hextechanalytics.com); riftboundfrance.fr's aggregate has Monastery of Hirana 82% / Forbidding Waste 76% / Star Spring 53%, and the two mobalytics lists run Forbidding Waste / The Arena's Greatest / Zaun Warrens — Forbidding Waste is the only pick all three agree on.
7. **Common mistakes** — Thin, and only what's written: (i) fighting before the fight is clean — "Movement alone does not add Might; a badly chosen fight can spend a card without creating XP" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); (ii) losing the first fight at all — "Without winning the first combat, the Legend does nothing at all" (riftboundfrance.fr) / "falls behind if early fights are lost" (riftbound.gg) — Tier 2, two sites; (iii) spending XP "on marginal value" instead of on the fight that sets up the next one, and misreading buff-vs-rescue as two abilities per turn when both Exhaust the Legend (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); (iv) buffing a unit that already carries a buff, since the +1 is lasting "only if the unit does not already have a buff" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2 — one site, and the exact Buff rule should be checked against the Core Rules before relying on it in a sanctioned event). **No verified basis yet** for anything more specific — Horror-timing errors, over-rescuing and handing back the point (section 5's Tier 1 caution is this book's derivation, not an observed pilot error), Void Assault target-selection errors — Tier 3.

**Worked example — Zed, Master of Shadows (Fury+Chaos), the Combo example, chosen because it's the one catalog entry whose whole identity rests on a named community deck ("Miracle Zed") rather than on Legend text — which makes it the sharpest test of the rule that a combo primer must describe the real line or say it can't, never rebuild one from card text — while its own catalog status is NOT CONFIRMED (the "two Champions = two lanes" split was denied; the single-engine identity held).** Like the examples above, this one is deliberately imperfect: no public source gives a scripted turn-by-turn card sequence, so section 2 describes the engine at the level real sources actually describe it and says where the script stops; section 3's only written mulligan guide is paywalled and is reported as absent; and the real results data cuts against the "Tier 1 / high Tier 2" rating the catalog carries — that disagreement is stated, not smoothed over.

1. **Identity** — A trash-fuelled aggro-tempo engine that fills its own trash (Burn / discard) to (a) turn 0-Might Shadow Clone tokens into Assault-4 attackers, (b) drop Rhasa the Sunderer for 0 Energy, and (c) rebuy units from trash with Last Rites, closing with explosive "Miracle" turns rather than a hold-and-go-off combo. The real shell across five lists (hextechanalytics.com Speyer 35th 2026-08-15; riftmana.com Shaown 2026-07-09 and WhiteHotD 2026-07-22; piltoverarchive.com fabudl v26 and Rifeli) agrees on 3× Seal of Discord, 3× Stacked Deck, 2–3× Death Mark, 3× Shadow Order Disciple, 3× Rhasa, 3× Noxus Hopeful (Legion), Fizz, Trickster, Last Rites (1–3), Star-Crossed, and Zaun Warrens + Minefield as battlefields, with Seat of Power the usual third (hextech's list runs Shadow Temple instead) (Tier 2); Chosen Champion is genuinely split — Without a Sound (hextech, Rifeli, Riftlab, both riftmana lists) vs. From the Shadows (fabudl, Moe, Legendori) — and pilots who pick Fury Zed say they rarely cast him ("I actually don't know if I've ever played this champion," eMOEtional; Tier 2).
3. **Mulligan targets** — **No verified basis yet** for a written keep/ship rule from a Zed pilot: the only "mulligan guide" named for this deck sits behind eMOEtional's Metafy paywall, and hextechanalytics.com, Rifeli's guide, and Riftlab all carry no mulligan section (Tier 3 for anything more precise than below). What is sourced (Tier 2, each single-source): eMOEtional (2026-08-09) says "you need a turn one play," runs eight 2-drops for that reason with Death Mark as the fallback turn-1 play, and — on triple Ride the Wind — "I'm going to hard mulligan for it"; 50k Comics' recorded openers (2026-07-20, pre-release) ship two Rhasas ("we'll probably never see them again, but... you pay the price to play") and a second Death Mark ("one copy is essentially two copies") to dig for a 2-drop; hextechanalytics.com adds only that Clones "need units in trash before their attack trigger adds meaningful Might," i.e. an all-payoff hand does nothing on turn 1–2. Translated through this book's three-part frame (Tier 1 synthesis): turn-1 play = one of the 2-drops (Shadow Order Disciple / Traveling Merchant / Treasure Hunter / Tideturner depending on list); turn-2 plan = a Burn or discard source plus, ideally, Ride the Wind for the double-conquer in line (a); line into the win condition = the trash count — a hand of Rhasas and Last Rites with no filler is the ship signal because nothing in it fills the trash that its own payoffs need. Which one card to send back from a hand that is only one enabler short is not sourced.
4. **Turn-by-turn priorities** — Early: "Fill the trash while developing enough units to contest a battlefield" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2); the deck "can typically threaten two points on turn two where a lot of other decks can't," and that early point lead is the plan (eMOEtional, Tier 2). Because scoring is checked at Beginning phase, the double-conquer turn only pays if the conquered battlefields survive the opponent's turn — the real answer isn't to defend them but to score-and-retreat ("You get two points, you move back to safety," Legendori 2026-08-13; Tier 2), which fits this book's timing rule by removing the target rather than guarding it (Tier 1 synthesis). Mid: "Turn three is typically the turn where you're playing out like one to two Rhasas" (eMOEtional; Legendori: "turn three or turn four"; Tier 2), and "Use a Clone's banish to empower Zed, then filter only when the replacement card is worth exhausting the Legend" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2). Closing: "Count what remains in trash before choosing between another Clone attack and a discounted Rhasa" (hextechanalytics.com, Tier 2) — the same trash card is fuel for three competing effects (Clone banish, Rhasa's discount, Last Rites' Recycle-2 equip cost), so the closing turn is a budgeting decision, not a "go off" button (Tier 1 synthesis of the three texts). Race posture: this is not a combo deck that stalls to assemble; every source above says fall-behind-and-lose — "if you fall behind in tempo it can feel really hard to get back... holding is pretty difficult" (eMOEtional, Tier 2).
5. **When to fight, when to hold** — Fight; the pilots are unanimous that this deck does not hold ("this deck does not want to be holding, you want to be assault swinging," Legendori; "protecting your board isn't your primary concern," Rifeli; Tier 2). But "pass last, spend last" applies *inside* the Showdown as the deck's real edge: the Clone's Assault-4 trigger is a "may" that resolves on attack, and Bushwhack (Hidden), Switcheroo (Hidden, Action) and Star-Crossed (Reaction) all work in the Combat Showdown Step — so as attacker with Focus first, move the 0-Might Clone in, let the defender commit their trick, then decide whether to banish for Assault 4 or Switcheroo the Clone's 0 Might onto their attacker (Tier 1 card text + this book's Showdown-Step ordering; the Switcheroo line is eMOEtional's, Tier 2). Perfect Execution is the exception — it has no Action/Reaction keyword, so its ready-plus-Assault-3 is a main-phase play made *before* the Clone moves in; the resulting Assault-7 Clone is Legendori's stated line and the pilot must commit to it before seeing the defender's response (Tier 1 card text; Tier 2 for the line). Two hold verdicts, both Tier 2: (i) the only real early hold is a hidden Switcheroo behind a Clone — swing for 0, swap Might, "now my unit is a really big attacker and they have a zero might defender" (eMOEtional); (ii) after Zed, Without a Sound conquers, pay 1 Energy + Chaos Power to swap him home with the Clone he just made — the Clone holds the point, "it demands the opponent to do something about the clone but it also keeps your Zed in safety" (Legendori). Since damage is assigned attacker-first and lethal-first, know the *post*-banish Might (Clone 4, or 7 with Perfect Execution, or Disciple's +1-per-move) before moving in, not the printed 0/2 (Tier 1).
6. **Common lines** — (a) 2-drop turn 1; turn 2 move it onto battlefield A, then Ride the Wind onto battlefield B ready, take both conquest triggers and two points, then move back to safety — "even if you went second, you're going to be at two points a lot of the time" (eMOEtional; Legendori describes the same sequence with a single unit; Tier 2, two independent sources). (b) Hide Bushwhack, then Death Mark: Burn 3, the Clone enters ready and attacks now, banishing one of the three just-burned units for Assault 4; if the burn turned up another Death Mark, Flow it (1 Energy + 2 Power) from trash for a second ready Clone and the Flow banish Empowers Zed for a loot (Riftlab; Rifeli "generate multiple attacking tokens with four Might"; 50k Comics shows the Flow-recast-then-Empower half on camera; Tier 2). (c) At 10+ trash: Rhasa for 0 Energy, Seal of Discord for the Power, Noxus Hopeful for 2 via Legion off the same turn's earlier play — Battering Ram (SFD-012) also drops to 1 in the Riftlab/riftmana builds (Riftbound Radar, Riftlab; Tier 2). (d) Last Rites on a 2-drop or a Clone early, then every conquest/hold replays a unit from trash — "late game getting Rhasas back for the seals" (Legendori; Rifeli; Tier 2). (e) Shadow Order Disciple onto A (Burn, +1) then Ride the Wind onto B (Burn, +1 again) = a 4-Might 2-drop that beats the format's early defenders (eMOEtional; Legendori "you can get up to like four"; Tier 2). **No further lines verified** — anything involving Shadowblade Lurker or Kennen's Flow-granting conquest is only described card-by-card, not as a sequence (Tier 3).
7. **Common mistakes** — Thin, one guide site plus scattered pilot remarks: (i) "Banish too much and later Clones or Rhasa lose fuel" — "assign every trash card a job before spending it"; (ii) treating Zed's loot as card draw when "discard-then-draw replaces a card but does not increase hand size" (both hextechanalytics.com, Vendetta, Tier 2 — one site's list). Two more, single-source Tier 2: (iii) forcing the 0-Energy Rhasa — "Don't get too caught up trying to play the card for zero energy every time... sometimes it's good just to play anyway for cheap" (Riftlab, 2026-07-24); (iv) hands and turns built around a payoff before the trash exists — Clones "need units in trash before their attack trigger adds meaningful Might" (hextechanalytics.com). **No verified basis yet** for anything more specific (e.g. Last Rites equip timing, when to loot vs. keep Zed Empowered as a threat) — Tier 3.

## Tournament procedure notes

This book is mostly card-agnostic mechanics, but a few procedural rules only matter in sanctioned play and are easy to never encounter until they matter:

- **Shortcuts are allowed, but they're a courtesy between players, not a rules exemption.** Tournament Rules 503: players may skip announcing every technically-precise step of a play sequence when both players understand what's happening — that's what lets a real game move at a normal pace instead of narrating each rule in full. 503.9.b (see the chain/priority section above) is one specific named shortcut: playing a card without separately declaring "and now I pass" is assumed to mean you passed, unless you say otherwise.
- **A triggered ability you don't acknowledge in time is treated as never having happened.** Tournament Rules 506: the player responsible for a triggered ability must acknowledge it by the point it would have an observable effect on the game — miss that window and the trigger is simply forgotten, not queued up for later. Doing this repeatedly to dodge a mandatory trigger you don't want is itself a penalizable pattern, not a free out.
- **Open-decklist events are a Head Judge's call, not a fixed rule of every event.** Tournament Rules 401.5: decklists are private by default; a Head Judge can choose to run an entire event, or just its final rounds, under an open-decklist policy where every player's list is public. Don't assume either way without checking that specific event's format.

## Process discipline

- **This book gives strategic judgment and rules background; detailed interaction analysis now belongs to `rule-consult`.** "Should I hold this Battlefield" or "how do I mulligan this hand" are this book's actual subject matter, answer them directly. "Who was right about what just happened" or "what is the ruling on this interaction" should route through `references/rule-consult/rule-consult.md`, which can give a cited most-likely answer with assumptions and confidence but still does not replace the event's Head Judge.
- Don't answer a non-obvious "what happens if…" interaction from card text plus general TCG intuition. Use the shared official source-precedence reference and the `rule-consult` contract; a community database such as `app.riftjudge.com` may support the analysis but is not official authority.
- A single bad Showdown doesn't diagnose a deck or a line of play as wrong — same discipline as the deckbuilding book's "don't judge after one or two games." Look for a pattern across repeated attempts before concluding a specific sequencing choice was the mistake versus just a bad matchup or bad luck on the Might comparison.
- When reviewing a play retroactively, separate "was this legal/did it resolve correctly" (`rule-consult`) from "was this the right decision given the information available at the time" (`deck-coach` or `player2-agent`). Don't let uncertainty about the first one leak into a strategy verdict.
- This book's core mechanics sections above are deliberately card-agnostic. "Piloting a specific decklist" is the one place this book does get archetype- and card-specific, and it inherits the library's card-pool discipline accordingly (see the library's top-level rule and the deckbuilding book's `regional-legality-model.md`): a tactic described by a source predating Riftbound's first ban wave (2026-03-31) may already be describing something banned everywhere, in every region, including ones that haven't launched yet. That section's Tier 2/3 split exists specifically to keep this contained — mechanical synthesis stays evergreen, sourced tactics carry the same staleness risk as everything else that names a real card.
